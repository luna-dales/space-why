Things I make

Making and baking

I’ve had the benefit of growing up among talented crafters, bakers, and artists. In my family, we’re encouraged to develop skills, sports, and passions we can enjoy throughout our lives.

Baking, crocheting, and metal jewelry making have become part of that story for me. They connect time spent learning from family with growing independence, more complex projects, and opportunities to teach and give back.

Baking

Baking has always been part of my life

Baking resonated with me from a very young age. I spent countless hours with my grandmother in the kitchen, learning through time together and practice.

Baking with my grandmother

Those early hours in the kitchen gave me a foundation to build on. Mixing, baking, and decorating became familiar activities, and each finished project gave me another reason to keep learning.

More of my early baking

Taking baking classes

By age ten, my parents were regularly enrolling me in adult baking classes to develop my techniques and independence in the kitchen. I also took an adult baking class at Milk Bar.

Working independently in a kitchen can be a real challenge with low vision. Classes and practice helped me keep developing that independence alongside my baking skills.

Cakes, cookies, and decorating

As my skills developed, my projects grew to include layered cakes and more detailed decorating. The cookies and cakes here show how I work with color, shape, and small details as well as the baking itself.

I regularly enter my baked goods in local fairs. I’m still chasing a “Best in Show” ribbon for my Almond Cherry Squares.

Now I get to teach

Today, I teach younger family members and am the keeper of family recipes and holiday food traditions. The time I once spent learning beside my grandmother now connects with the time I spend helping the next generation in the kitchen.

Crocheting

My sister got me into crochet

My passion for crochet grew from my sister’s interest. It became a creative activity we could share. She specializes in traditional blankets, while I focus on three-dimensional amigurumi figures.

Learning to crochet

Crocheting gives me something I can work on a little at a time. Practice with the hook and yarn is the foundation for building shapes and bringing a project together.

Albinism InSight

What helps me crochet with low vision

My crafting was featured in Albinism InSight. I’ve also shared my crochet adaptations on NOAH’s website, describing how practice and a few changes to my tools help me work more independently.

  • I use a long Tunisian hook for ordinary crochet because its length makes it easier to grip and locate.
  • Bright yarn and colorful stitch markers help me see my work and track where rounds or rows end.
  • A large tapestry or ballpoint needle helps me join the crocheted pieces.
  • I read patterns on my computer using dark mode and magnification. On paper, colored pens help me mark completed steps and add notes.

Read my contribution to NOAH’s Low Vision Tips & Tricks.

Some of my smaller projects

Small projects let me explore rounded forms, color changes, and recognizable details. A whale, a frog, or an ear of corn gives those repeated stitches a shape and personality.

Animals and other things I’ve made

More involved figures bring several elements together: bodies, ears, faces, limbs, and other small features. The collection shows a progression in the kinds of forms and details I can combine, from rounded objects to characters with multiple parts.

A few more crochet photos

Sharing crochet through NOAH

I’ve donated crocheted items to help raise funds for NOAH, and I teach younger NOAH members to crochet at summer camp. Something that began as a shared interest with my sister has become a way to share with a wider community.

Handcrafts can be adapted for people with low vision. Learning to make things myself, and helping someone else learn, are both part of why these skills matter to me.

Jewelry

From bracelets to wire jewelry

My jewelry making has grown from simple bracelets to more complex metal pieces. The progression is in the techniques: arranging beads and charms, shaping wire into a wearable form, then combining curves, wrapped joins, and decorative elements.

Starting with bracelets

Simple bracelets offered a starting point for choosing colors, arranging beads, and adding charms. Working on a small piece helped me practice handling individual parts and thinking about how they look together.

The group photo shows the range of bracelets we shared around the table, while the workshop video gives a glimpse of making jewelry together.

Learning to shape wire

Wire brought a different challenge: shaping the material itself. Instead of arranging pieces along a strand, I could form an open outline with curves and spirals. The side view of the arm piece shows how those bends create a shape that follows the arm.

Putting it all together

The finished arm piece brings those skills together. A shaped wire frame provides the outline; finer wire wraps around sections of it, and butterflies and hanging flowers add detail. The design asks me to think about the whole shape and the small connections at the same time.

That is the progression I enjoy: starting with a simple arrangement, learning to shape the material, and combining more techniques in one piece.

Things I want to keep doing

These interests connect the people who taught me with the people I can now help. Whether I’m keeping a family recipe, making an amigurumi figure for a fundraiser, teaching a younger maker, or shaping a piece of jewelry, I’m building skills I can keep using throughout my life.
