WhereIsIt

Finding an item in a grocery store should not depend on being able to read every label on a crowded shelf.

WhereIsIt is an iPhone app I've been developing to help people with low vision find grocery products. It combines AI-assisted recognition of shelf photographs, crowdsourced product locations, and accessible ways to search and hear results.

The prototype works end to end: a user can photograph a shelf, review recognized products, save their locations, and search for those products later. I have also tested viewing a contributed shelf photograph across two different iPhones.

The idea

From a shelf photo to a usable location

01 / Contribute

Photograph a shelf

A user selects the store, aisle, and side, then takes or chooses a photograph of grocery products.

02 / Check

Review AI results

The app asks an AI model to identify products and estimate where they appear. Users can correct names or remove mistakes before saving.

03 / Find

Search and locate

Another shopper searches by product name or brand, sees its recorded location, and can view the shelf image or hear the location aloud.

Product positions are estimates based on contributed photographs. A shopper's review is important because AI recognition is not always correct.

Designed around a simple task

The home screen separates the two main tasks: contributing shelf information and finding a product. Search results show the recorded location, while a separate view displays the original shelf photograph for visual reference.

The settings screen gives users control over text size and appearance. Speech input and spoken location information provide alternatives to reading small product labels.

Accessibility by design

More than a visual search result

Because I have low vision, I'm interested in software that offers different ways to access the same information. WhereIsIt explores how familiar phone features can support the everyday task of locating groceries.

  • Speak to search: Search for products using voice input.
  • Hear the location: Have recorded aisle and shelf information spoken aloud.
  • VoiceOver: Navigate the app with Apple's screen reader.
  • Text and appearance: Adjust in-app text sizes and select light or dark appearance.
  • Shelf photographs: Use a visual reference when it helps, rather than relying only on an aisle number.

These features work in the prototype, but broader testing with people with different visual abilities is still a future goal.

Engineering behind the app

Connecting iOS, AI, and a shared database

What happens behind the scenes

The SwiftUI app sends a shelf photograph to a Supabase Edge Function. The function calls OpenAI's GPT-5.4 model to identify visible grocery products and approximate their positions. The results return to the phone for review.

Approved observations are saved in PostgreSQL, and photographs are stored in a private Supabase Storage bucket. A product-search view combines product and store data so another authenticated user can retrieve a location and its linked photograph.

Supabase authentication and access policies control requests to the database and stored images. The project is still a prototype, so security and deployment procedures need additional review before a broad release.

Engineering lessons

Making a working prototype meant solving real problems

Challenge / 01

Recognition needs review

AI can identify many products in a photograph, but errors in names and product positions are possible. I built a review step so people can correct or remove suggestions before adding them to the shared database.

Challenge / 02

Sharing photos securely

A product observed on one phone needs to be visible on another. During testing, one phone could view its own shelf photo while the other received an “Object not found” error. We traced this to a Supabase Storage policy, corrected the permitted read operations, and verified that the photo appeared on both phones.

Challenge / 03

Making results usable

Finding a product is not only about showing its name on a screen. I incorporated voice search, spoken location details, VoiceOver, and text-size choices so people can use more than one way to access the same information.

The prototype has been tested on two iPhones. That is an early functional check, not a substitute for usability testing with a broader range of shoppers.

Iteration and next steps

What still needs work

Building a useful prototype has also made the limitations clearer. AI can recognize many products, but busy shelves make exact product boundaries and positions difficult to estimate consistently.

My next goals include improving bounding-box accuracy, testing product matching across different packaging and photos, making the app easier to use with large text and speech, and strengthening privacy and reliability before considering wider use.

One important part of the design is allowing people to correct AI-generated identifications before the observations enter the shared database. The application should treat AI as a tool that assists shoppers, not as a source that is always right.
